<p>Look at the top performers on any floor and ask how they arrived. A surprising number came through someone already on the team. A friend, a cousin, a former coworker who heard about the job over dinner. Owners love these hires and treat them as luck. They are not luck. Referrals are a system with three parts, and most operations run zero of them on purpose.</p>
<h2>Part one: the ask has to be specific</h2>
<p>Know anyone who wants a job is not an ask. It is noise. The ask that works is narrow. Who is the one person you would want next to you on a hard shift? Who do you know that is stuck in a job that does not pay them for effort? Specific questions make people picture a face. Once they picture a face, they have a name, and the name is the whole game.</p>
<h2>Part two: the rep has to look good for it</h2>
<p>A referral is a rep putting their reputation next to someone else's. Make that a good bet. Reply to the referred person within the hour, treat them well, and tell the rep what happened, whether it was a yes or a no. A rep who refers someone and hears nothing back never refers again. A rep who watches their friend get a fast, respectful process refers three more.</p>
<h2>Part three: reward the behavior, not just the hire</h2>
<p>Most bonus structures pay when the referred person hits day ninety. Fine, keep that. But also recognize the ask itself, in front of the team, the day it happens. Recognition is faster than money and it teaches the room what you want. Money confirms it later.</p>
<h2>Why it compounds</h2>
<p>Referred hires show up already knowing what the job is, because a friend told them the truth. They have someone on the floor who wants them to make it. They stay longer, ramp faster, and refer in turn. Run the three parts for one quarter and the pipeline changes shape, from strangers to people who were vouched for.</p>
<blockquote>Luck is what you call a system you have not written down yet.</blockquote>
