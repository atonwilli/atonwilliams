<p>I used to think the best opportunity won the candidate. Better pay, better training, better story. Then I looked at where the people we lost actually went, and most of them went to whoever called first. Not the best job. The first job.</p>
<h2>Applying is a moment, not a decision</h2>
<p>When someone hits apply, they are in motion. They are hopeful, a little nervous, and open. That window lasts hours, not days. Every hour you wait, someone else fills it. By the time your recruiter gets to the call sheet on Thursday, the person who applied on Monday has already sat through two interviews and picked one. You never had a shot, and you never knew it.</p>
<h2>What speed says about you</h2>
<p>A reply inside the hour tells a candidate three things before you say a word. This company is organized. This company wants me. This company moves. Those are the same three things they will want to believe about the job on their hardest week. A reply on day three says the opposite, and no amount of pitch on the call undoes it.</p>
<h2>Speed is a system, not a personality</h2>
<p>You cannot hire a recruiter who is fast enough to do this by hand. The application has to trigger the first message on its own, the reply has to book a slot the same day, and the reminder has to go out without anyone remembering to send it. The human enters when there is a human conversation to have. Everything before that should already be done.</p>
<p>Once that is true, your recruiter's day changes. Instead of chasing a list of people who have moved on, they walk into a calendar of people who already said yes to a time. Same recruiter, same job post, twice the shows.</p>
<h2>The number to watch</h2>
<p>Time to first contact. Not applications, not interviews, not hires. Those follow. If you only track one recruiting number this month, track that one, and treat every hour of it as a candidate you handed to someone else.</p>
<blockquote>The candidate does not compare offers. They compare who showed up.</blockquote>
