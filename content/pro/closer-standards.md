# The Closer's Standards, Pro

## Start here: the build prompt

Paste everything below into Claude. Fill in CONTEXT. It writes your pre-handle lines, your follow-up library, the conditional-yes script, and your commission plan, from your real objections and your real numbers.

```
You are building a closer's operating system for one rep or one team. Read CONTEXT, then THE STANDARDS, then follow BUILD. Ask me up to five questions first if CONTEXT is thin. Use only my facts. No pressure tactics, no invented claims.

CONTEXT
- What we sell and the verified facts: [ ]
- Medium: [ ]
- The twenty objections we hear most, in the customer's words: [ ]
- What our pitch says today about price, timing, and commitment: [ ]
- Our follow-up channels (text, email, call) and the typical gap between conversations: [ ]
- Commission structure, and my last six months of commission if I want the money plan: [ ]
- Words we use: [ ]   Words we never use: [ ]

THE STANDARDS
In the conversation: pre-handle instead of rebutting (raise the doubt first, in your words, inside the pitch); leave with a hard yes, a hard no, or a conditional yes with a date; never send "just following up"; proof is the whole thing (show, do not describe); practice the moment, not the movie. With the money: no sales math (record month times twelve is not a salary), no vanity math (the story lasts a day, the bill lasts a year), protect the days after a heavy night, stack the skill before the trappings, respect the boring layer under any tool you build.

BUILD
1. PRE-HANDLE LINES: for each of my twenty objections, the line that raises and answers it inside the pitch before the customer says it, and where in the pitch it goes (open, story, show, ask, lock).
2. THE FOLLOW-UP LIBRARY: twelve messages for my channels, each naming what the customer said the decision depends on, what changed, one small next step, and a date. Label which situation each is for.
3. THE CONDITIONAL YES: the script for turning "let me think about it" into a yes with named conditions and a hard time to meet them.
4. THE HARD NO EXIT: two sentences that take the no, keep the relationship, and leave one door open.
5. THE WEEKLY AUDIT: ten yes-or-no questions in my words.
6. THE COMMISSION PLAN (if I gave numbers): my six-month average, the monthly budget built on it, the savings rule for anything above it, and the purchase test (does this buy a use or a story).
7. QUIZ MODE: twenty short conversation endings; for each, the standard that was broken and the line that would have fixed it.

Rules: plain language, no hype, no dashes, mark assumptions [CHECK].
```

Everything after this explains the method behind what it builds.

## 1. Pre-handling, in practice

Count the objections you rebut on a normal conversation. Each one is a line missing from the front of your pitch. A rebuttal library of five hundred answers is a monument to problems you keep choosing not to fix. Pre-handling is three moves: name the doubt before they do ("most people ask about the price right here, so let me put it on the table"), answer it in one line with a fact, and move on without waiting for agreement. Done inside the pitch, the objection never arrives.

## 2. The conditional yes

"Let me think about it" is a no you have not heard yet, unless you turn it into conditions. "Of course. So you are thinking about the right thing: is it the price, the timing, or whether it will actually work?" Then: "If [condition] were true by [date], would this be a yes?" A yes with a condition and a date is a real follow-up. Anything else is a hard no, and you take it with grace.

## 3. Follow-ups that carry weight

Five lines. You said the decision depends on X. Here is what changed since we spoke. Here is what happens next. By when. One question, answerable in a word. Never "checking in." Never "just following up." If you have nothing new to say, you have nothing to send, so go get something new.

## 4. The money

Sales math: record month times twelve. Vanity math: the story is up for a day and the bill lasts a year. The four days: a heavy night costs the rest of the week's sharpness and you will not be able to point at the reason. The fix is one rule: budget on the average of your last six months, and everything above it is savings. The purchase test: does this buy a use or a story?

## 5. The stacking skill

Sales compounds. Each rep, each book, each coached call adds to a base that returns non-linearly after year five. One hour a day beats a weekend binge. Do not skip the daily hour for the flashy win. Income, freedom, leverage, equity, hiring, and negotiation all sit downstream of the ability to get another person to agree to a transaction. Get good at that first.

## 6. The boring layer

If you build yourself a tool, know what real software takes: support, testing, monitoring, backups, access control, and a data model. A prototype that runs is not a product that survives month three. Use tools that already have the boring layer and spend your hours on the skill that pays.
